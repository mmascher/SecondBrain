# Conditions Preparation Workshop 2026 — Validation Procedures, Tier 0 Outlook, and Future WMS Discussion

## Summary

An afternoon session of the Conditions Preparation Workshop 2026, with three talks captured in full and a fourth beginning as the transcript ends. Marcos Sales presented the current fast track and full track [[Conditions Preparation|conditions]] validation procedures and the request form used to trigger them, followed by Q&A on automating and streamlining validation for Phase 2. Dmytro Kovalskyi gave a Tier 0 outlook for Long Shutdown 3 and Run 4, covering the staffing gap, prompt-reconstruction latency/storage trade-offs, data retention, and site efficiency for remote prompt reconstruction. ## Decisions / Conclusions

- The review of the current CMS workload management system (WMAgent/"WM4") that started in 2024 concluded in 2025: the outcome was that the current system **is not sustainable for Phase 2**. The system has been frozen since September 2025.
- After evaluating alternatives (PanDA from ATLAS, and DIRAC/DiracX used by LHCb and other experiments), the future CMS workload management system for Phase 2 will be built on **[[DIRACX]]** (the next-generation [[DIRAC]]).
- The scope of the future system will be broadened to cover **all** major CMS workflow classes in one system: Tier 0 workflows, Monte Carlo simulation, data reprocessing, and distributed user analysis ([[CRAB]]) — replacing the current split across CRAB, WMAgent/WMCore, and the Tier 0 WMAgent.
- Tier 0 operations/development staffing: starting in 2027 there will be **no dedicated support for Tier 0 operations and development**, expected to last until at least 2029 (Dmytro could not give a firmer end date); the previous Tier 0 operator role has been redirected to work on integrating/building the new workload management system.
- Full track validation typically takes **3–5 days, sometimes more**, to complete.
- Prompt reconstruction delay: **48 hours remains a reasonable/realistic target for Run 4**; pushing to **7 days** is "probably doable" but requires substantially more buffer storage (see Discussion for the petabyte estimate) and depends on final rates and available resources.
- Current disk-retention practice for specialized datasets (e.g. AlCaReco-type outputs) is roughly **30 days or less**; longer retention is handled through case-by-case exceptions rather than a general policy change (for now).
- Data recall without special permission: any CMS user can recall up to **0.5 PB** overall (or **50 TB** for a specific site), retainable for **up to 6 months**; some users have permissions to recall more.

## Discussion

### Conditions validation procedures (Marcos Sales)

Marcos Sales, together with a co-convener of the Conditions Preparation validation effort (name transcribed unclearly, possibly "Sachewata"), presented the two validation paths used before new conditions/calibrations go into production:

- **Full track validation** — the standard procedure, used in the regular case. Scope includes calibration tests and HLT reconstruction. It requires release-validation ("ReVal") jobs, submitted through the team's own tooling, that compare the target conditions against a reference. The contact must: upload the new constants to the conditions database, create global tag candidates, post details of the new conditions in [[CMS Talk]] (CMS Talk / hypernews-style forum), and fill in a request form (the transcript consistently renders this as the "**alcohol**" form/ticket — almost certainly a mis-transcription of "**AlCa**", i.e. the Alignment and Calibration request form; preserved here as transcribed uncertainty). After a run-validation job (ReVal) is submitted, DQM plots are posted to a Jira ticket for the relevant DPG/POG(s) to review; after their approval and a final green light from the "L2 [convener] of condition preparation," the new conditions are moved to production.
- **Fast track validation (FTV)** — a more automated, dedicated workflow for cases needing a faster turnaround. Described as roughly four steps: tag the conditions and create global tag candidates for Express and Prompt; a tool compares target vs. reference global tag differences and runs the validation; after a green light, the contact uploads the validation payloads; finally there is an official announcement that the new conditions/global tag are approved, including the start run/date. The manager (Marcos or his co-convener) opens the tracking ticket; the HLT-side contacts for review are described as the "TSG" and (transcribed) "DTM" groups — exact group names uncertain in the transcript.

Operational notes: for full track validation, the Express global tag field is normally not needed; CPU/memory fields can usually be left at default; a notes field exists for anything non-standard (e.g. a specific required HLT menu). Marcos stressed that filling in the AlCa/"alcohol" form completely speeds up validation because it gives the team what is needed to auto-schedule the ReVal + DQM steps as a single automated workflow; if a contact needs a non-default HLT menu for their ReVal jobs and doesn't say so, the default menu will be used and the validation may not actually exercise the intended configuration. A new tool is in development (not yet in production) with a similar user interface to the current one, aimed at integrating the AlCa/archival request tooling with another job-submission tool (name not recalled by the speaker) at the back end. First-time requesters can reference existing example fast track and full track validation requests.

Marcos noted the team is discussing possible improvements to the full track validation procedure to address issues that sometimes slow it down, but this is still in discussion and, if adopted, would not change the core steps a contact needs to follow to request a validation.

### Q&A after Marcos's talk — toward Phase 2 automation

- A DPG-perspective comment (attributed in the transcript to a name rendered inconsistently as "John" and then "Thomas" — identity unclear) raised that for a given FTV Jira ticket it is often not immediately clear which DPG/POG is expected to give feedback, since tickets are addressed to a general group. Proposed, as a brainstorm rather than a settled plan: templating FTV tickets by request type/structure (while still allowing more people to be added), a mechanism to assign one specific responsible person recorded in the ticket, and a visible "traffic light" (and possibly response-time tracking) to drive the FTV flow — floated as a possible target for "Run 4" automation work.
- The moderator agreed this is a fair point: today the whole FTV workflow relies on people self-identifying as needing to give feedback, rather than the system explicitly pinging the people who must respond; the main previously-identified sources of delay are people not reacting promptly once jobs/results are ready. Streamlining this was called out as needed "in whatever form the validation procedures will exist for Phase 2."
- A participant (name transcribed uncertainly as "Matthiasz") asked what the Phase 2 plans are for speeding up or even removing human supervision from validation, given it can become a bottleneck. Response (speaker attribution unclear): fast track validation is already fairly automatic (it mainly relies on someone triggering the run), so it can more or less easily be further automated; full track validation needs to be assessed case by case, since different condition types require feedback from different people/groups.
- Arun Kumar noted that for full track validation, a "release-validation" style of automation could be pursued, but this would require talking to the offline-validation conveners; this work is understood to stay with the offline validation group and its own tooling rather than be folded into the automation framework under discussion. He reiterated the team's goal of avoiding delays and said known causes of delay are understood and being worked through one by one.
- A longer exchange (multiple contributors under the VCROOM149 label) explored how the automation framework relates to validation more broadly: it currently produces "cloud validation" plots (used already in Run 3) for internal DPG information and to help build FTV tickets, but is not itself part of officially validating deployed conditions. One proposal floated: if the new automation framework puts the full conditions workflow "in code" (PCL through to global tag), payloads would go straight into the conditions database, so it would need to be decided upfront which conditions require a full, detailed study (at least once) versus which are simple, repeated updates of an already-validated payload type needing only a lighter automatic check (e.g. that the new value doesn't break reconstruction of other data, not just that it is a valid number). The idea discussed was two layers of validation: an automatic per-payload check that every DPG/group defines and that must pass before anything is written to the conditions DB, and — for cases still needing human judgment (e.g. calibrations) — an automated production of validation plots with a final human confirmation ("flag") before deployment, rather than manual production of the plots themselves. It was agreed that defining what test is "enough" for a given condition type must come from DPG consensus first; once defined, adding the check to the automation framework would just be "another conditional in the loop." This was expected to be part of commissioning and to evolve/be adjusted early on.

### Tier 0 outlook for Run 4 (Dmytro Kovalskyi)

- **Staffing**: from roughly 2027, there is no dedicated support for Tier 0 operations/development, likely until 2029 (date uncertain — dependent on hiring). The Tier 0 operator's effort has been redirected to help build/integrate the new workload management system; since Tier 0 is, in principle, "just a specialized workflow" on top of any workload management system, Tier 0-specific work can resume once that system exists. First availability of the future workflow management system is expected around **2028**, initially focused on Monte Carlo production, with Tier 0 work expected to follow.
- **Express processing**: used roughly 7% of CPU resources at CERN in Run 3; more could be used in principle, but is constrained by available CERN CPUs across sites.
- **Prompt reconstruction latency and storage**: one of the most sensitive areas — extending prompt-reconstruction delay by one additional day costs roughly one extra petabyte of buffer space at end-of-Run-3 rates, and Run 4 rates should be assumed to be at least a factor of 2 higher. 48 hours remains realistic; pushing to 7 days is "probably doable" but was estimated to need roughly 5 extra petabytes of space versus the 48-hour baseline, and depends on final rates/resources. Rolling calibration is expected to become more important given increasing data volumes. Processing must always have a defined time limit — unlike central production, Tier 0 cannot wait indefinitely for a calibration issue to resolve; the system must always know what to do when time is up. Dmytro floated (explicitly as an unconfirmed idea) the possibility of different timing "routes" per primary dataset for channels needing extra-precise calibration (e.g. H→γγ was used as an example), noting this would add complexity.
- **Site efficiency for prompt reconstruction outside CERN**: Tier 1 sites in Europe with dedicated networks (e.g. Fermilab) showed little to no degradation vs. running at CERN in Run 3, but efficiency drops for sites without a dedicated network, due to delays accessing data remotely (worker nodes idling). Heavy-ion reconstruction (a heavier workload) showed consistently higher inefficiency at remote sites; Run 4's higher pileup may push CMS toward using more remote sites, though not expected to reach heavy-ion-scale track multiplicity. Given limited CPU (not just disk), maintaining today's level of remote prompt reconstruction may not be easy; parking some data (with associated delay before it's reconstructed/available) is likely depending on rates.
- **Scouting**: identified as a workflow Tier 0 could usefully post-process (e.g. object IDs, more complex taggers, possibly jet-energy-scale corrections in a "rolling" style) to offload CPU-intensive work from HLT. HLT scouting rates were ~20 kHz in Run 3, reaching ~60 kHz for low-pileup running in 2025/2026; Run 4 may reach ~750 kHz — an order of magnitude increase and likely one of the major workflows Tier 0 will need to handle.
- **NanoAOD**: increasingly critical for analysis, DPGs, and POGs. Tier 0 has CPU capacity to produce it, but not as fully independent workflows for every case — CMS will need to agree on a more compact way of running it, or accept the delays of sending it to central production instead, which currently has no mechanism to guarantee timely delivery (only Tier 0 currently offers timely delivery guarantees).

### Q&A after Dmytro's talk

- Arun Kumar asked for confirmation on the 7-day buffer figure; Dmytro clarified it refers to the extra buffer storage such a delay requires (roughly 5 extra petabytes versus the 48-hour case, scaled from the ~1 PB/day estimate at Run 3 rates), and that changes don't have to apply uniformly — the system already handles data by primary dataset, so different timing could in principle apply to different channels (again floated as an idea, not a plan).
- On data retention for specialized datasets (currently ~30 days or less on disk): Dmytro said exceptions can be arranged for specific use cases (some groups already have dedicated longer-retention policies, e.g. a year), but noted CMS has generally been producing "too much" calibration data, and Run 4 will require more discipline about space use for calibration outputs generally (whether or not they go to tape). He stressed that CMS as a whole needs to internalize that not everything will remain available on disk and that recalling from tape carries expected delays — people need to request data ahead of need.
- On recall mechanics: any CMS user can recall up to 0.5 PB in general (or 50 TB targeting a specific site) and keep it for up to 6 months without special permission; some users have elevated permissions for larger recalls; recall can also be done via CRAB, but central production's recall is always request-driven — there's no mechanism to proactively/automatically guarantee timely recall.
- Arun noted full track validations are often stuck in "staging" when pulling data from tape. Dmytro explained that "stuck in staging" usually means ~99% of the needed data is already on disk and the process is waiting on a small number of remaining tape files; users should get comfortable proceeding with a large-enough available fraction rather than expecting 100%, since long-tail files are increasingly hard to guarantee (they may eventually complete on their own, or — if a tape is corrupted — the file may be invalidated). Arun described the team's existing practice of arranging short-term (2–3 month) targeted transfers of data to CERN disk when DPGs pre-warn them of an upcoming validation; this hasn't always happened in time, causing stuck workflows, and the team is working on improving it. Dmytro reiterated that for anything on a critical timeline, someone needs to plan ahead and request the recall explicitly, since central production only recalls on request and any manual operation takes time.
- Arun asked why remote sites are less efficient reading data from CERN. Dmytro attributed it mainly to delay introduced by non-dedicated networks (Fermilab, with a dedicated network, performs essentially perfectly despite distance); heavy-ion's consistently higher inefficiency reflects its heavier reconstruction workload, and Run 4's higher pileup may push CMS toward a similar pattern (though not to heavy-ion scale).

### CMS workload management system status and Phase 2 plans (Alan Malta Rodrigues)

Speaking on behalf of the CMS workload management team, Alan reviewed the current system: [[CRAB]] (distributed analysis and private Monte Carlo) and [[WMCore]]/[[WMAgent]] (Monte Carlo simulation and data reprocessing) as the two current workload management systems, plus a specialized "flavor" of WMAgent for Tier 0, which includes shorter-latency components that acknowledge new streamer files from the storage manager. He also described the two workflow "topologies" currently supported (sequential, and a fork-style topology producing multiple outputs from one task) and the "stepping vs. chaining" distinction for data locality — independent jobs vs. fully dependent jobs run within a single grid job to keep data dependencies on the worker node — noting the goal for Phase 2 is to support hybrid combinations of the two.

Alan then covered the future plans (see Decisions above for the review outcome, DiracX choice, and broadened scope). Requirements gathering has been underway for months across multiple groups/organizations, organized around four pillars: workflow lifecycle and policy, data awareness (locality, placement, redistribution, retention), Tier-0-ready execution (full support for current Tier 0 workflows is planned, not just a freeze), and the resource model (using constrained resources wisely). The system's architecture is still evolving and is waiting on Architectural Design Records (ADRs) from the DiracX core developers, expected within days to weeks of the meeting; those will inform CMS-specific design decisions. [[HTCondor]]/[[glideinWMS]] was identified as a core component used in Runs 1–2 — the unified resource pool mechanism that matches jobs to resources, managing the CERN pool across [[WLCG]] Tier 1/Tier 2 sites plus a portion of HLT nodes.

On timeline: the system must be ready for Run 4, though no firm date could be given since design is still in motion; **2028** is a checkpoint tied to the CSA, where the system should demonstrate some (not full) capabilities and be "on the right path" for Run 4; Alan separately estimated the DiracX-based system is unlikely to exist in a real, usable form before **mid-2027**.

### Fitting conditions workflows into the future WMS — case study and discussion

Building on a document and prior meetings/email exchanges with Thomas, Antonio, Jan, and others, Alan walked through open questions about how three conditions-related workflows might fit into the future WMS:

- **PCL (prompt calibration loop)**: a multi-step workflow with streamer files as input at lumisection granularity (source uncertain for Run 4 — possibly still Storage Manager) and multiple outputs at different stages (AlCaReco, AlCaPrompt, etc.). Open questions raised: is periodic AlCaHarvesting still needed, or multi-run harvesting instead; what triggers AlCaHarvesting; is DQMGUI still the right target for harvested output, or should files go elsewhere (e.g. EOS); and what are the real per-step latency requirements (figures of ~18 and ~48 hours were mentioned from prior slides but which applies to which step needs confirmation).
- **Multi-run harvester**: similar open questions to AlCaHarvesting — same requirements? how is payload-to-grid-job assignment decided? what happens to the output (upload target, storage)?
- **Automation framework**: understood to need "all the flexibility possible," which Alan flagged as hard to design for concretely. Key unknowns: expected input/output granularities, and which external systems it needs to interface with (e.g. DBS, a "run registry," and other possible data sources) — plus full data management (retention, placement, replication) for data that isn't already tracked in [[Rucio]].

A substantial discussion followed about how literally the future system should mirror these existing workflows. One view (attributed to the VCROOM149 room, speaker unclear) cautioned against trying to "mimic" all three legacy workflow types feature-by-feature, and against simply demanding unconditional "flexibility" without grounding it in concrete use cases — calling flexibility alone "a distraction" unless scoped. The counter-view (also unclear attribution, engaging directly with Alan) was that the future system doesn't need to replicate current mechanics exactly, only to fulfill the underlying requirements and flexibility needs; Alan proposed, for any given requirement, three possible resolutions: it's naturally supported by the future WMS as-is; it needs an extension/component (something Tier 0 workflows may also need); or it's different enough that supporting it would require substantial dedicated additional effort. A participant noted the group had already run some technology tests and would value something usable on a near-term timeline to assess fit; Alan reiterated there's currently nothing concretely usable beyond early prototypes, since the DiracX architecture itself isn't finalized (pending ADRs), with mid-2027 as a rough earliest estimate and CSA 2028 as the capability checkpoint.

A related, higher-level concern (raised by a participant under the VCROOM149 label) was that the relationship between the calibration automation framework (which NGT works on) and the future WMS needs more centrally coordinated planning — it wasn't obvious whether Conditions Preparation should design the calibration framework and adapt it to run within the WMS, whether the two efforts should stay separate, or whether some requirements should be jointly developed; the uncertain WMS timeline makes it hard for the calibration side to plan or start testing. Alan invited the group to reach out to the WMS team and set up follow-up discussions "in the coming weeks," mentioned an existing recurring meeting ("Monday's meetings") as a possible venue for occasional updates, and noted an upcoming workshop on "the archives" around the end of October that should give a clearer picture of direction. He also noted that WMCore/"Dublin Core" being a CMS-specific solution (supported by very few people) was itself part of the motivation for moving to a community-backed project like DiracX, which multiple experiments support long-term.
