# New WM Dev Team Weekly Meeting

## Summary

The team prepared content for an upcoming CMS Offline Computing Week presentation comparing three candidate workload-management directions — [[DIRAC]], [[PanDA]], and continuing to build on [[glideinWMS]]/[[HTCondor]] — as part of the ongoing evaluation of alternatives to [[WMCore]]/[[WMAgent]]. The meeting opened with an extended discussion (Valentin Y Kuznetsov, Andrea Piccinelli, Alan Malta Rodrigues, Eric Vaandering, Marco Mascheroni) about what message/metrics the presentation should convey to the CMS Level-1 decision makers, converging on cost of ownership as the key objective metric. The group then reviewed slide content topic by topic (system architecture, data management, supported workflows), discussed Tier 0/PromptReco scope with Antonio, and finished with a detailed walkthrough of Kenyi Hurtado Anampa's PanDA/glideinWMS integration-strategy document, where Marco raised concerns about architectural over-complexity ("two engines") echoing earlier objections to WMCore.

## Decisions / Conclusions

- No formal decision was reached on what "impacts" and "conclusions" to present for each system; Andrea Piccinelli said this would be decided later, once the group reaches that point in the slide deck, and proposed doing it collaboratively.
- Valentin Y Kuznetsov argued that simply listing features found for each system (DIRAC/PanDA/glideinWMS) has little decision-making value, since he assessed development effort as roughly equal in magnitude across options; he proposed **cost of ownership** (maintenance cost + operational cost + development cost) as the key objective metric that should anchor the presentation's conclusions. Andrea Piccinelli agreed this could be added to the existing slide set (the point was not disputed by the group), but did not commit to a specific metrics methodology.
- Alan Malta Rodrigues cautioned that treating the three systems as interchangeable ("point A to point B") is an oversimplification, since it's not yet established that each provides the same outcome for CMS; he also stated CMS should regard these systems' communities as partners/potential co-developers, not just as clients/consumers.
- For the data-management slide, the group concluded that DBS-related work must be implemented regardless of which system is chosen (all systems will need DBS interaction for discovery/injection), so DBS itself was excluded from the system-comparison discussion; only Rucio-related metadata/handling questions remain relevant to the comparison.
- Eric Vaandering assessed that the handling of unmerged files is "done" for the comparison purposes: both PanDA (via ATLAS's approach) and DIRAC put unmerged files into Rucio, though the specific transfer mechanism differs — DIRAC apparently uses its own tools to move files but calls "Rucio Upload" only to register them, while PanDA uses "Rucio Upload" to move files but a separate "Rucio injector" to register them. Eric's assessment was that CMS's/ATLAS's current approach (contacting the Rucio server, similar to CMS's site-conf-based stage-out) is preferable, citing a known incident where DUNE overloaded the Rucio server by having all worker nodes call "Rucio Upload" directly.
- The group agreed not to add further detail to the data-management slide about worker-node metadata injection into Rucio or output-data-cleanup optimization (raised by Duong Nguyen), judging these not critical to the Offline Computing Week comparison, though Alan Malta Rodrigues noted output-data placement/cleanup is more a matter of experiment policy than system capability.
- Regarding PanDA/glideinWMS integration, Kenyi Hurtado Anampa's document lays out three candidate strategies ranging from staying highly dependent on PanDA's existing components (Strategy 1) to minimizing dependence on PanDA components in favor of CMS-controlled pieces (Strategy 3, closest to what Kenyi previously proposed for DIRAC). Kenyi's conclusion for this meeting was that all three strategies are technically feasible ("is it feasible to integrate glideinWMS with PanDA?" → yes), and that choosing among them is a separate decision to be made afterward, not part of the feasibility answer itself.
- Marco Mascheroni stated a preference for Strategy 3, since it most fully replaces PanDA's provisioning/scheduling layer with glideinWMS rather than running both, but voiced a strong concern that layering glideinWMS on top of PanDA's existing Harvester/job-brokerage/job-database components (Strategies 1/2) risks recreating the same "two engines"/over-engineered, hard-to-evolve architecture that originally motivated moving away from WMCore. Valentin Y Kuznetsov backed this concern, arguing that a layered ("plugin on top of") approach still creates ongoing maintenance cost (API compatibility, package/OS alignment with the upstream project) even if it avoids fully duplicating a component.
- Kenyi Hurtado Anampa clarified that under Strategy 2 ("recycling Harvester" with plugin-based submission to Condor), Harvester's site-provisioning functionality itself would not be duplicated — CMS would only replace the job-creator/job-submitter components (the WMAgent-equivalent pieces) with a CMS/glideinWMS adapter, while the rest of PanDA's Harvester continues to do provisioning; Marco disputed that this avoids duplication, since CMS would still be running both HTCondor/glideinWMS and PanDA/Harvester layers for related purposes.
- Kenyi Hurtado Anampa noted that progressing from Strategy 1 toward Strategy 3 over time appears feasible with no major architectural blockers identified so far, allowing an incremental path rather than requiring an immediate full commitment to Strategy 3.
- Kevin Lannon emphasized that many of the maintenance-cost/divergence concerns raised depend heavily on the collaboration model that would be established with PanDA or DIRAC developers (e.g., joint maintenance agreements), and that DIRAC and PanDA should be evaluated separately rather than letting concerns about one generalize to a blanket rejection of collaboration with either.
- Andrea Piccinelli clarified the actual scope of Kenyi's assigned investigation: the original ask from Level-1 was not "what is the most maintainable way to adopt PanDA" but rather "if we want to keep using HTCondor/glideinWMS together with PanDA, what would that take, and is it worth it" — reflecting an existing baseline preference (fed back previously) to continue using HTCondor/glideinWMS. If that baseline changes, Andrea said someone would need to inform Level-1 that CMS is open to dropping HTCondor/glideinWMS if a more maintainable solution results.
- Marco Mascheroni raised a "provocative question" — if Harvester alone can already submit pilots and acquire resources, why keep the glideinWMS/Condor layer at all when using PanDA outside of Strategy 3? Alan Malta Rodrigues offered a partial answer: PanDA's single global pool (no separate per-site queues) is seen as a genuine advantage attributable to glideinWMS/Condor's matchmaking model.
- The team ran out of time to discuss the DIRAC integration document in the same depth and deferred it; Kevin Lannon and Kenyi Hurtado Anampa indicated availability constraints for a follow-up the next day.

## Discussion

### Framing the presentation for Level-1 decision makers

Valentin Y Kuznetsov opened with an extended argument (using a car-buying analogy) that simply outlining discovered features for each candidate system is not useful for Level-1's decision, because he judged development effort to be roughly comparable in magnitude across DIRAC, PanDA, and a CMS-led glideinWMS-based system. He argued the group should instead present the **consequences** of each choice, particularly cost of ownership (development + operational + maintenance cost), since that is what remains different and decision-relevant after accounting for roughly equal development effort. He also raised, as a secondary concern, that adopting an external system may not be "generic" enough in practice — citing his own investigation of DIRAC's database layer and schema coupling, and LHCb's need for experiment-specific plugins (e.g., "bookkeeping") — meaning CMS might end up maintaining a CMS-specific DIRAC layer not meaningfully more unified than a CMS-built system, undermining the alignment/community benefit of adopting an existing system.

Andrea Piccinelli responded that the team had already agreed on a topic-by-topic, side-by-side slide structure, and that conclusions/impacts/metrics would be discussed together once the group reaches that part of the deck — he did not want to over-invest in resolving the metrics question at this point in the meeting.

Alan Malta Rodrigues added several points collected while listening: (1) assuming the three systems produce the same outcome for CMS is an oversimplification requiring more scrutiny of how/whether each actually provides what CMS needs; (2) the team should think of these systems' communities as partners/collaborators, not merely clients — a partnership similar to Rucio's model, where CMS has both core Rucio developers and developers serving CMS-specific needs; (3) cost of ownership is likely to end up similar across options regardless of capability differences, so the two main high-level metrics are cost of ownership and the "investment"/community-relevance angle — related to broader concerns (raised in prior meetings) about funding sustainability for a CMS-only ("homemade") solution.

### Incentive/community-adoption debate (Valentin, Eric, Marco)

Valentin Y Kuznetsov questioned what incentive an existing team (e.g., PanDA developers) would have to adopt CMS-requested features, given their systems already work for their own use cases after many years, and reiterated skepticism that DIRAC (or PanDA) is truly "generic" based on his code-level investigation. Eric Vaandering agreed this is a valid concern but framed adopting an external system as partly "a leap of faith," requiring an assessment of whether a team genuinely wants to build a community project versus merely tolerating outside users; he drew on his own experience with [[Rucio]] and the CERN tape system. He noted Rucio was designed from the start to be agnostic/generic, which he was less sure is true of PanDA or DIRAC.

Marco Mascheroni contributed an analogy (which Valentin endorsed as capturing his point well): Rucio's success across experiments came from all experiments sharing a common underlying engine (FTS), whereas for workload management, glideinWMS, DIRAC, and PanDA each have their own distinct job-running "engine" — so combining approaches risks "building a car with two engines," creating conflict rather than a unified system unless the underlying design questions are worked through carefully.

### Data management slide review

Alan Malta Rodrigues summarized the data-management topic (Rucio and DBS) and metadata handling was noted as previously split conceptually between DBS (data provenance: run/lumi/event) and Rucio (data transfer/positioning/storage; potential enrichment with prep ID, campaign, or other metadata). The group concluded DBS-related implementation work is required under any chosen system, so it was excluded from the comparison; only Rucio-related handling remains in scope.

Eric Vaandering explained the mechanics of how unmerged files are handled: both PanDA/ATLAS and DIRAC put unmerged files into Rucio, but via different mechanisms — PanDA uses "Rucio Upload" to move the file but a separate "Rucio injector" component to register it in Rucio, while DIRAC moves files with its own tools but uses "Rucio Upload" only to tell Rucio the file has arrived at its destination. Marco Mascheroni asked specifically whether these systems contact the Rucio server directly from the worker node (as CMS does via site-conf for stage-out protocol selection) or use another mechanism; Eric confirmed ATLAS/CMS-style server-contact is used by ATLAS, and noted his gut assessment that this approach is better, citing that DUNE previously overloaded the Rucio server by having all worker nodes call "Rucio Upload," a problem Eric said is now resolved. Marco noted CMS's own approach benefits from CVMFS/Squid caching for site-conf lookups.

Duong Nguyen raised whether the future system needs to address optimizing input/output data cleanup, particularly output datasets that must remain fully staged at a destination for extended periods before cleanup. Alan Malta Rodrigues judged this likely not high priority, though relevant to understand (e.g., PanDA's "tape carousel" mechanism, which keeps a low disk footprint fetching from tape, though he was not fully certain of its behavior). He characterized output-data placement/cleanup timing as more dependent on experiment policy (workflow execution/priority/handoff decisions) than on the underlying system's inherent capability. Alan noted Panda can (as an apparently optional mechanism, per his understanding, to be corrected by Eric) automatically move "pre-merge" data to a specified destination once available, triggering merge jobs there; Duong questioned whether block-by-block cleanup during placement is possible in PanDA/DIRAC, which was not resolved in the meeting.

Todor asked what "Rucio for private analysis workflows" refers to on the slide; Andrea Piccinelli explained it concerns whether CRAB users can (as historically explored) use Rucio to move their output data to preferred storage locations after workflow completion, or whether they need another mechanism. Vijay added that CRAB currently uses FTS for this, and that Rahul had investigated using Rucio for CRAB more fully in the past, but that effort was shelved and Rucio is not currently used for this in CRAB "in the full way we can."

Eric Vaandering also noted, regarding data movement into worker nodes, that DIRAC does not appear to pre-stage input datasets on a job-by-job basis the way ATLAS/PanDA does (ATLAS creates a dataset per job's inputs and ensures it is at the running site beforehand); Eric said he was not certain DIRAC does any equivalent input pre-staging outside of the transformation description level. Todor added, based on his understanding (subject to confirmation), that DIRAC can achieve equivalent data movement by configuring a transformation step in a production chain that specifically triggers a call to the data management system to move data from point A to point B before the next step — i.e., handled at the workflow/transformation-configuration level rather than as an inherent system feature.

### Tier 0 scope and PromptReco reliability (with Antonio)

Alan Malta Rodrigues asked whether Tier 0's scope (currently repacking, Express, and PromptReco) would necessarily stay the same as CMS evaluates these systems, suggesting scope could shift depending on which system is adopted. Antonio reported that he had recently learned PromptReco carries more importance for calibration workflows than previously assumed: PromptReco produces early information used to refine detector-condition parameters, which are only "perfected" through repeated processing; skipping/delaying PromptReco means recalibration effectively restarts a year later than intended, at re-reco time. He said the underlying goal being pursued is reducing the need for re-recall/re-reco by making PromptReco more reliable, though he was relaying a summary he had only received a couple of hours before the meeting and deferred nuanced technical/organizational judgment to people above his level.

Andrea Piccinelli pushed back that, from a resource/scheduling perspective, all jobs (Tier 0 or central production) ultimately land on the same Condor infrastructure, so changing which "highway" (system/path) is used to reach Condor does not inherently change Condor's own scheduling behavior for Tier 0 vs. Monte Carlo jobs — implying the calibration-timing concern may be more about current-system priority/share design than an inherent constraint of a future system. Marco Mascheroni suggested that Tier 0's resource share may currently be controlled at the SI (site infrastructure) sharing layer while actual scheduling happens at a workflow/scheduler layer above it, which could explain why people are describing constraints specific to the current system rather than a future one. This scope question was not resolved; Antonio and Andrea agreed to continue the discussion separately, outside this meeting.

### PanDA/glideinWMS integration strategy walkthrough (Kenyi's document)

Marco Mascheroni framed his review around three recurring questions he wanted answered by Kenyi Hurtado Anampa's document for both PanDA and DIRAC: (1) where site/queue definitions (CE, queue, cores/memory/whole-node, pilot config — CMS currently has ~300 such glideinWMS entries) would live, and whether the layer that manages this would be imported from PanDA/DIRAC or kept modular/replaceable; (2) how and where global fair-share (e.g., Tier 0 vs. Tier 1 production/analysis shares) would be defined and enforced, and who makes scheduling decisions; (3) how resource-provisioning decisions (e.g., not submitting pilots for unused GPU resources) would be made, and whether this would duplicate existing glideinWMS/Condor logic. Marco also flagged credential management across systems as a related but lower-priority concern not covered in depth this meeting.

Kenyi Hurtado Anampa described the guiding idea as keeping matchmaking-related functionality with glideinWMS/Condor, via a CMS-specific plugin at the PanDA job-brokerage level (acting similarly to WMAgent, before jobs are materialized in the Condor queue), while glideinWMS's factory/frontend continues to handle pilot matchmaking/scheduling and credentials. He outlined a spectrum of three strategies: **Strategy 1** keeps all Panda components (highest dependency on PanDA, includes running the PanDA pilot which fetches jobs from the PanDA server); **Strategy 2** ("recycling Harvester") reuses Harvester with a CMS/Condor-backend adapter — CMS builds only a job-creator/job-submitter replacement (analogous to injecting Condor ClassAds for site targeting), letting glideinWMS handle pilot scheduling from those ClassAds, with the PanDA pilot still running as the application layer inside the glideinWMS pilot; **Strategy 3** minimizes dependence on PanDA components, taking fuller CMS control, at higher implementation effort — closest to what Kenyi previously proposed for DIRAC. He stated all three appear technically feasible, and that choosing between them is a separate decision from establishing feasibility; he also noted the option to progress incrementally from Strategy 1 toward 3 with no identified major blockers.

Marco Mascheroni challenged Strategy 2 specifically, arguing that keeping Harvester (mirroring glideinWMS's factory function) alongside a separate glideinWMS factory, plus separate job databases in both PanDA and the Condor scheduler, constitutes duplicated "engines" and is a red flag reminiscent of the very over-complexity that motivated moving away from WMCore in the first place; he warned this could produce an "over-engineered system that we cannot evolve." Kenyi clarified that under Strategy 2, Harvester's provisioning functionality specifically would not be duplicated (glideinWMS would fully handle that), and only the WMAgent-equivalent job-creator/submitter piece would be replaced by CMS/glideinWMS — Marco remained unconvinced this avoids the underlying duplication concern.

Valentin Y Kuznetsov reinforced Marco's concern, arguing that even a "layered" (rather than duplicated) architecture still creates real, non-trivial ongoing maintenance cost, since CMS would need to track upstream API/package/operating-system compatibility with whichever system it layers onto, and CMS's requirements might diverge from e.g. ATLAS's over time, forcing either difficult alignment or growing divergence. He tied this back to the original motivation for reconsidering WMCore (excessive complexity) and cautioned the group against arriving, via a different path, at a similarly over-engineered outcome.

Kevin Lannon noted that many of these maintenance/divergence concerns depend on what collaboration model is actually established with PanDA/DIRAC developers (e.g., an explicit agreement to jointly maintain code and avoid divergent choices), and that without knowing that mode of collaboration in advance, a technical evaluation is inherently incomplete. He cautioned against generalizing PanDA-specific concerns to DIRAC, saying the two should be evaluated independently. He also raised an open question about whether adopting a layered Strategy-3-like approach with PanDA would be an intermediate step toward a jointly evolved future architecture (e.g., PanDA/CMS agreeing to converge around glideinWMS as a shared engine) versus a permanently separate CMS-specific branch — which would have different long-term effort implications.

Kenyi Hurtado Anampa also noted that his original plan to build an entirely new "CMS job submitter" component turned out to be unnecessary after talking with PanDA developers — the same outcome can reportedly be achieved by disabling certain configuration knobs in the existing Harvester, which is why his current design reuses ("recycles") Harvester rather than replacing it.

Marco Mascheroni raised a related idea (not pursued further in the meeting): rather than fully replacing Harvester with glideinWMS, it might be possible to find convergence by merging/evolving Harvester and the glideinWMS factory together, since he sees the two components as functionally similar (both make provisioning decisions based on data).

Alan Malta Rodrigues summarized the trade-off spectrum as running from solutions relying on common/shared components to solutions relying on CMS-customized modules integrated into PanDA, noting this raises the question of whether adopting PanDA is even worthwhile if CMS ends up building extensive custom layers (Strategy 3). He suggested a possible unlisted "hybrid" option: a common workload-management solution not specific to any one experiment, potentially achieved via closer integration ("handshake") between Harvester and glideinWMS, maintained by a broader community rather than by CMS or ATLAS individually — reflecting the earlier discussion about reusing shared solutions for problems CMS shares with others. He also flagged, without elaborating (having forgotten a planned point), that the group is missing dedicated inspection/R&D into glideinWMS's own architecture and possible database use (e.g., use of a "SCAD" database not currently used) that could apply regardless of which system CMS ultimately adopts.

Marco Mascheroni posed a deliberately provocative question to the group: if Harvester alone can already submit pilots and acquire resources, why keep the glideinWMS/Condor layer at all under strategies other than Strategy 3? He said he regretted raising it in a larger/public meeting context and would prefer resolving such questions internally before Offline Computing Week, expressing concern that CMS risks presenting strategies it already suspects are too difficult to maintain without having done the deeper technical evaluation needed before the March presentation.

Andrea Piccinelli responded that the investigation's actual scope, as originally requested by Level-1, was not "what is the most maintainable way to use PanDA" (which would likely favor PanDA alone, without glideinWMS) but specifically "what would it take to keep using HTCondor/glideinWMS together with PanDA, and is it worth it" — reflecting an existing baseline preference, previously communicated back to Level-1, to continue using HTCondor/glideinWMS. He said that if this baseline preference should change, someone needs to explicitly tell Level-1 that CMS is open to dropping HTCondor/glideinWMS in favor of a more maintainable alternative; that has not yet happened.

Alan Malta Rodrigues offered a partial answer to Marco's provocative question: PanDA's advantage of using a single global pool (no separate per-site queues) is itself attributable to the glideinWMS/Condor-style matchmaking model, making it a genuine point in favor of retaining that approach.
